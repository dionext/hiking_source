# Hiking on the Dachstein plateau (southern side)

Here is an account of my small-scale exploration of the central Dachstein massif. Before reaching it, we toured major Austrian cities: Vienna, Graz and Innsbruck. There was no time left for Salzburg, so we chose the region's famous Hallstatt, in Salzkammergut at the foot of the northern Dachstein slopes. A few kilometres from Hallstatt, a cable car carries most visitors to the plateau's Five Fingers viewing platform. It is expensive, but on a clear day the view makes up for it. As a mountain enthusiast, I had long wanted to climb onto the Dachstein on foot and considered several options.

The first and most obvious option is an ascent on the northern side from the lower cable car station. That was my plan on my first visit, but the local tourist office explained that mid-May was still too early: too much snow remained to explore the plateau properly. Instead, I climbed nearby Sarstein, across the valley. It is lower than the Dachstein, yet even its plateau was covered in knee-deep snow in May.

The second option is a long ascent from Lake Gosau on the northwestern side. Unless you are exceptionally fit, there is not enough time to go up and down in one day. There is, or was, a hut on the plateau for such trips. Conditions are basic, it opens around mid-June, and the price is a little below that of an inexpensive village hotel. Such huts are generally not listed on hotel booking sites: you must find their phone numbers and arrange a bed directly, possibly in German. This is common practice in the Alps. The Dachstein plateau has several huts, plus the more comfortable Lodge am Krippenstein near the upper cable car station. The latter can be booked through ordinary booking services, but is more expensive.

The third option, which I chose, is to ascend the southern slopes from Ramsau. There is also a cable car here, leading to another viewing platform and a suspension bridge described as Austria's highest. From there you can walk towards the glacier. Trails around this cable car allow an independent ascent from the foot of the mountains, but walkers may find the eastern side of Ramsau more interesting; the cable car is to the west. The eastern routes are quieter and less developed. I had three full days in Ramsau, and favorable weather allowed three ascents on different routes, each taking a full day.

Some background from the Internet: "Dachstein is a karst massif and the second-highest mountain in the Northern Limestone Alps. The high plateau spans Upper Austria, Styria and Salzburg, hence its description as the mountain of three provinces. The massif measures about 20 by 30 km, with around ten peaks above 2,500 m; the highest are in the south and southwest. Glaciers descend from the northern side below the summits, while the southern cliffs drop almost vertically into the valley. Its exceptional landscape led to its inscription, together with Hallstatt, on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1997. The central Dachstein forms a striking landmark, its 1,100 m southern walls rising abruptly above the valley. The sharp-crested main ridge includes the massif's highest summit, Hoher Dachstein, at 2,995 m above sea level."

How do you reach the southern slopes? Schladming, served by a railway station, is a famous ski resort and regular World Cup venue. It is also a good hiking base, but for another part of the mountains. Reaching the central Dachstein requires another forty minutes by bus, so if that is your main goal, staying in Ramsau is more convenient.

Timing: reasonably comfortable hiking probably begins in June, or preferably July, though accommodation then costs more. The area is often rainy, which must be considered, but in my experience summer rain does not last for weeks on end and there are usually windows for hiking.

Apart from the railway station–Ramsau center–cable car line, regional buses are infrequent, so it is best not to depend on them.

Here are the routes I walked. [http://maps.kompass.de/](http://maps.kompass.de/) provides respected Alpine maps showing the route numbers used below. My three walking days were:

Day 1. Route 616 starts at the bus stop opposite Hotel Berghof. This is the most recommended route to the plateau, following a good trail that is difficult to lose. Near Guttenberghaus hut it branches; I followed 618. A further branch joins parallel route 673 along the crest of a sheer cliff. It looked interesting, but I did not attempt it alone. Behind the ridge I reached the 2,489 m point, Edelgnebhohe. There was plenty of snow in places, especially on shaded northern slopes. I did not consider avalanches a danger there, but a slip could easily lead to a twenty-metre slide. I wanted to reach the upper cable car station, or at least the descent on 672, but snow and ice made that trail very poor. I instead took a connector from the 2,489 m point to the lower part of 672. Although a walking route, the descent is steep, with iron handrails and cables in many places. I kept looking at the opposite cliff, trying unsuccessfully to see the upper part of 672; perhaps it no longer existed. At the bottom I checked the timetable and hurried to the lower cable car station, just catching the last bus to Ramsau. Otherwise, it would have been at least an hour's walk.

Day 2. Route 619 ascends through the popular Silberkarklamm gorge. The start can be reached by bus or on foot, first along the road and then through the forest, following the green marking for cycle route 20. Entry to the gorge trail is paid; it follows a river with small waterfalls. Several via ferratas are visible on the surrounding cliffs, and diagrams are available when buying the ticket. Route 7 along the gorge slope is a free but more difficult alternative. Both trails meet near Silberkarhütte, where a moderately difficult ascent to the plateau begins. I then followed 618 and descended by familiar route 616. Do not miss the view of the beautiful mountain lake on the plateau.

Day 3. Routes 666 (oops!) and 667. Reaching the start requires a fairly long walk along the road. The area is much quieter than Ramsau, and an advertisement obscured part of the route on the simple map I borrowed from the tourist office. GPS frequently failed in the gorge, making this the most difficult day for navigation. I never established exactly which trail I used to ascend. However, most of the route is marked, and hikers occasionally pass who can help with directions. From the plateau I returned on 618 and again descended on 616.

Something I would still like to do is traverse the whole massif, for example on route 616, ascending from the south and descending to the north, or vice versa. This would require accommodation on both sides of the Dachstein or in mountain huts. Returning to the starting point by public transport the same evening is impractical: the journey takes considerable time, and transport stops running at night.
